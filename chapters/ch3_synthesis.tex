Chapter 1 introduced the established results and the conjectures with proofs.
Chapter 2 tested small cases and searched larger ones for dense examples.
This chapter brings those strands together.
It identifies the main conclusions and separates the central open problems from questions of classification and completeness.

\section{The directed frontier}
The simple directed arc problem has several verified dense constructions, but
their feasibility must not be confused with optimality. A construction proves
a lower bound: it shows that at least that many arcs are possible. Proving the
extremal value would additionally require an upper bound for every feasible
digraph.

\begin{construction}[Competing dense directed families]\label{const:directed-comparison}
For $n\ge m\ge2$, choose the densest of the directed hub, the augmented
bipartite digraph and the clique-core digraph
(\Cref{const:directed-hub,const:augmented-bipartite,const:clique-core}).
Their respective arc counts are
\[
 H_m(n)=m(n-1),\qquad
 Q_m(n)=\floorfrac{(n+m-2)^2}{4},\qquad
 B_m(n)=\floorfrac{(n+m-3)^2}{4}+2(m-1).
\]
These three letters name arc \emph{counts} of the three directed constructions
rather than the digraphs themselves. Two similar symbols elsewhere name graphs:
$H_{m,n}$ is the undirected hub-and-spokes graph of \Cref{const:hub-spokes},
which carries $\floor{m(n-1)/2}$ edges and is distinct from the directed hub
count $H_m(n)$ above, and $B_{p,q}$ is the one-directional complete bipartite
digraph of \Cref{const:bipartite}.
Each of the three directed constructions defining $H_m(n)$, $Q_m(n)$ and
$B_m(n)$ has maximum local arc-connectivity $m-1$. Consequently,
\[
 \ell_m^{\mathrm{dir}}(n)\ge \max\{H_m(n),Q_m(n),B_m(n)\}.
\]
This is a lower bound, not a proposed formula for the optimum.
\end{construction}

Which of the three families is largest depends on the range.
The clique-core construction of \Cref{const:clique-core} leads whenever
$m\ge5$ and $m+7\le n\le3m-3$. At $m=5$, $n=12$ it has $57$ arcs with maximum
local arc-connectivity $4$, against $56$ for the hub and the augmented
bipartite counts. Outside that window one of those two is largest, and for
fixed $m$ the augmented bipartite family is eventually the largest of the
three. That comparison ranks these constructions and nothing else, since
another feasible family could be denser still.

The displayed maximum is therefore a lower bound only. It is not claimed that
$57$ is optimal at $n=12$, $m=5$, nor that the maximum of the three counts is
optimal at any order.
At $m=2$, the proposed all-order value is given in
\Cref{thm:dir-arc-m2-exact}: it predicts that the bipartite count is optimal for $n\ge7$.
\Cref{fig:crossover} compares the hub and augmented bipartite counts at $m=3$.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{figures/directed_crossover.png}
    \caption{The hub and augmented bipartite lower bounds at $m = 3$, and their maximum. The plot asserts no upper bound.}
    \label{fig:crossover}
\end{figure}

At $m=3$, exhaustive search proves the values $6,9,12,15$ for $n=3,4,5,6$.
The two separation conditions agree at every one of these orders (\Cref{rem:dir-vertex-m3}).
The search does not settle $n=7$.

The earlier decomposition conjecture has been removed as well.
It would force the quadratic upper bound as soon as that branch exceeds the
hub bound, which already happens at $m=5$, $n=12$.
The counterexample therefore refutes that structural assertion too.

For fixed $m\ge3$, the augmented bipartite count has the same leading term and the same order of error as the optimum.
The proof of \Cref{thm:dir-arc-linear-error} gives an elementary
route-counting argument for this behaviour. The asymptotic statement also
follows independently from Huang and Lyu \cite{HuangLyu26} and the displayed
lower construction, as explained in \Cref{sec:status-simple-directed}.
The linear coefficient remains unknown among the results proved in this thesis.
Even identifying it would leave the exact bounded correction to establish.
More precisely, the construction gives a linear contribution $(m-2)n/2+O_m(1)$ above $n^2/4$, while the proposed elementary upper bound allows $4(m-1)n$. These inequalities do not show that a unique limiting linear coefficient exists.
Once $n$ is large in terms of $m$, the Tur\'an theorem of Huang and Lyu \cite{HuangLyu26} lowers the upper end to $m-1$ (\Cref{ch:basecases}).
The next section considers the separate question of extremal shape.

For fixed $m\ge3$, the directed vertex problem has the same leading term and error order by the same literature bound, and \Cref{thm:dir-vertex-linear-error} supplies a proposed elementary proof.
\Cref{app:proofs} treats it separately from the arc problem.
It remains open whether the two values are exactly equal for $m \ge 3$.

Allowing parallel arcs leads to the proposed exact formula of \Cref{thm:dir-multi-full}, whose proof remains unverified.

\section{Why direction makes the value quadratic}

In an undirected graph, every edge contributes to the degree of both endpoints.
Mader's theorem turns this symmetry into the linear cap $\floor{m(n-1)/2}$.
A digraph has no corresponding symmetry.

In \Cref{const:bipartite}, all arcs go from one part to the other and none return.
A directed route can therefore cross between the parts only once.
The construction has $\floor{n^2/4}$ arcs at $\lec^{\max}=1$.
No undirected graph of connectivity one can have quadratic size.
The linear cap does not survive the introduction of direction.
At $m=2$ and $n=8$, the one-directional construction has $16$ arcs while the hub has $14$.
Thus the hub cannot be optimal at that order.

\Cref{const:augmented-bipartite} extends this idea to every $m$.
Each vertex of the larger part receives $m-2$ extra in-arcs from within that part.
\Cref{fig:augmented-wall} draws the construction.
The added arcs provide $m-2$ short detours for each relevant pair.
They raise $\lec^{\max}$ to $m-1$ and the arc count to $\floor{(n+m-2)^2/4}$.
At $m=3$ and $n=10$, the construction has thirty arcs while the hub has twenty-seven.

The quadratic term is what the directed upper bound has to account for.
For eventual exactness, the argument must explain why no configuration has more
arcs than the augmented bipartite construction once $n$ is sufficiently large
in terms of $m$. The clique-core examples show why a restriction on $n$ is needed.
\Cref{prop:dir-arc-stability,thm:dir-arc-linear-error} address this structure in two ways.
In \emph{size}, the extremal value lies within $O_m(n)$ of the wall's count.
The remaining uncertainty includes the linear coefficient and the bounded correction.
In \emph{shape}, the proposed bipartisation bound says every feasible digraph becomes a subgraph of a one-directional bipartite graph after deleting $O(\sqrt{m}\,n^{3/2})$ arcs.
This weaker error comes from an argument that does not induct on a low-degree vertex.
The undirected problem has no analogous obstacle, because Mader's degree-counting argument closes it in a few lines.
The quadratic phenomenon is therefore the reason the directed and undirected problems differ in difficulty.

\subsection{Simple undirected graphs}
\label{sec:status-simple-undirected}

For edge separation, the answer is established at every order:
\[
 \ell_m(n)=
 \begin{cases}
 \binom n2,&n<m,\\
 \floor{m(n-1)/2},&n\ge m.
 \end{cases}
\]
When $n<m$, even the complete graph cannot contain $m$ edge-disjoint routes
between a pair. Once $n\ge m$, Mader's upper bound is attained by the
hub-and-spokes construction. The Gomory--Hu double count explains the role
of simplicity: at most $n-1$ graph edges have distance one in the tree, and
every other edge is charged to at least two tree cuts
(\Cref{thm:mader,thm:extremal-existence}).

Vertex separation permits a larger feasible family. Classical results show
that the edge formula remains exact for $m\le4$. At $m=5$, the values agree
through $n=13$, but the vertex maximum is $\floor{8n/3}-4$ from $n=14$
onwards. In particular the first gap is $33$ against $32$ at $n=14$.
This difference concerns maxima over all graphs, not merely a pair of
vertices with different local connectivities
(\Cref{thm:leonard,thm:sorensen-thomassen}).

For $m\ge6$ the exact vertex value remains open. The proposed block
formulation of \Cref{thm:simple-vertex-blocks} would express it as a knapsack
over feasible $2$-connected blocks and identify a limiting rate $c_m$.
The classical density and construction bounds motivate
$m/2<c_m\le2(m-1)$ under that formulation. The finite block sweeps inspect
small building blocks, but the larger recursive constructions in the
literature show why those sweeps cannot determine the general rate.

\subsection{Undirected multigraphs}
\label{sec:status-multigraph}

With edge separation, multiplicity removes the simple graph's distance-one
restriction. The established value is $L_m(n)=(m-1)(n-1)$, attained by a
tree with each edge repeated $m-1$ times (\Cref{thm:multigraph-edge}).
The upper bound counts cuts, so the conclusion holds regardless of the
shape of a feasible multigraph.

The vertex problem counts edge copies as separate routes. It therefore does
not reduce to the simple vertex problem. For a fixed simple support,
\Cref{lem:multi-vertex-split} separates parallel one-edge routes from longer
detours. At $m=2$ this forces a forest with multiplicity one and gives
$K_2(n)=n-1$. The proposed general $m=3$ value is $2(n-1)$, using the
unresolved incidence-rank argument. Finite exhaustive values agree with it.
At $m=4$ agreement with the edge value is observed through eight vertices,
but no general equality is established here. At $m=5$ direct exhaustion
already gives $K_5(4)=14>L_5(4)=12$.

The proposed block reduction explains how a favourable small support could
be repeated at larger orders. Thickened clique bouquets and complete
bipartite blocks lead to proposed bounds of order $m^2n$ when
$m\to\infty$ and $n/m\to\infty$. The proposed lower coefficient is
$3-2\sqrt2$ and the proposed upper coefficient is $2$, and neither a common
limit nor the best blocks are established. The nine-vertex sweep computes
$g_6(9)=54$. Interpreting that block score as the full value $K_6(9)$ uses
the conjectural block reduction and must retain that dependency.

\subsection{Simple undirected hypergraphs}
\label{sec:status-simple-hypergraph}

For an $r$-uniform hypergraph, one edge can connect several vertices but can
serve only one route in an edge-disjoint family. The edge bound is
\[
 \ell_m^{(r)}(n)\le\floorfrac{(m-1)(n-1)}{r-1}.
\]
Both the cut-induction proof of the bound and the attainment proof are
established. The stated attainment range is
$m-1\le\binom{n-2}{r-2}$: there must be enough distinct hyperedges to realise
the degree-capped construction (\Cref{prop:hyper-edge,thm:simple-hyper-edge}).
The range matters. At $n=r=m=3$ only one simple hyperedge is available,
whereas the displayed bound is two. Separately, exhaustive enumeration gives
the exact edge value eleven at $(n,m,r)=(6,6,3)$, below the bound of twelve.

For vertex separation, the $m=2$ value is established:
$k_2^{(r)}(n)=\floor{(n-1)/(r-1)}$. Feasibility is equivalent to the
incidence graph being a forest, and a star hypertree attains the count.
At $m=3$, the proposed incidence-rank inequality gives the bound
$\floor{2(n-1)/(r-1)}$, and simple attainment for $2<r<n$ is established.
Checking the final deduction does not settle the unverified rank inequality.
At $m\ge4$, an edge upper bound cannot be transferred using
$\kappa\le\lambda$, because vertex feasibility is the weaker restriction.
The higher-threshold vertex problem therefore remains open even in ranges
where the edge formula is attained.

\subsection{Undirected multihypergraphs}
\label{sec:status-multihypergraph}

The cut bound is unchanged when hyperedges repeat, but the available
constructions change. A star hypertree with each edge repeated $m-1$ times
reaches $(m-1)(n-1)/(r-1)$ whenever $(r-1)$ divides $n-1$. At $n=r=m=3$,
two copies of the only triple realise a value unavailable to the simple
model. Repetitions can also help outside that divisibility range: the
twelve-copy construction at $(6,6,3)$ exceeds the simple edge optimum eleven
(\Cref{const:multihyper-six}). Its assertion of optimality additionally
requires the cut bound above.

For vertex separation, repetitions do not help at $m=2$, because two copies
of one hyperedge already give two separated routes between its vertices.
The established value is again $\floor{(n-1)/(r-1)}$. The proposed $m=3$
upper bound is the same as for simple hypergraphs, and the repeated star
supplies the additional attainment cases stated in
\Cref{prop:hyper-vertex-lower-multi}. At larger thresholds, finite witnesses
and the simple-model lower bounds remain available, but the edge upper bound
does not establish vertex optimality. In particular a twelve-copy edge
witness is also a vertex lower bound, without proving the vertex maximum
is twelve.

\subsection{Simple directed graphs}
\label{sec:status-simple-directed}

Direction changes the scale: the one-way bipartite digraph already carries
$\floor{n^2/4}$ arcs with maximum connectivity one. At $m=2$, the exact
value under both separations is $M(n)=\max(2(n-1),\floor{n^2/4})$.
The lower constructions are explicit and the general upper argument rests on
the reachability-skeleton bound. Completed enumeration confirms the value
under both separations through $n=7$.

For fixed $m\ge3$, both maxima have asymptotic form
$n^2/4+\Theta_m(n)$ independently of the elementary proofs here.
To apply Huang and Lyu \cite[Theorem~2]{HuangLyu26}, set $t=m-1$.
Either feasibility condition forbids $m$ two-step paths with common
endpoints, since distinct midpoints give internally vertex-disjoint paths.
Their theorem therefore gives, for sufficiently large $n$,
$|A|\le n^2/4+(m-1)n+O_m(1)$. The augmented bipartite construction gives
$n^2/4+(m-2)n/2+O_m(1)$ from below. The contribution of
\Cref{thm:dir-arc-linear-error,thm:dir-vertex-linear-error} is a
self-contained elementary proof with an explicit upper bound valid at
every $n$, rather than a new asymptotic consequence unavailable from the
literature.

The exact value and the linear coefficient remain unresolved. The
clique-core example disproves the earlier hub-versus-bipartite formula,
and ranking the three displayed families does not prove that any eventually
wins over every feasible digraph. The two separations agree in the completed
$m=3$ computations through $n=6$. Whether they agree generally for $m\ge3$
is a further question, because an arc upper bound alone does not constrain
the larger vertex-feasible family.

\subsection{Directed multigraphs}
\label{sec:status-directed-multigraph}

For arc separation the proposed exact formula is
$L_m^{\mathrm{dir}}(n)=(m-1)M(n)$. The proposed proof removes a
reachability-preserving subgraph at each level of the route cap. The
thickened bidirected tree and balanced one-way bipartite graph provide
matching lower constructions. The general upper bound, fractional
integrality consequence and classification for $n\ge\max(8,2m)$ retain
conjectural status. Historical solver checks cover small orders, but the
unfinished $n=7$ computation supplies no independent upper bound.

For vertex separation, multiplicity and longer detours can favour different
constructions. Direct enumeration gives $K_6^{\mathrm{dir}}(3)=24$, whereas
the arc candidate at that order has twenty arcs. Thus the arc construction
is not even a universal finite optimum for the vertex problem. The proposed
leading term is $(m-1)n^2/4+O_m(n)$, with proposed lower constructions from
thickened complete digraph blocks at small orders and the bipartite family
at large orders. The exact correction and the most effective block shapes
remain open (\Cref{cor:dir-multi-incidence,prop:dir-multi-vertex-blocks}).

Search performance is a separate issue from these mathematical claims. At
$n=12,m=6$, all three equal-allocation arc searches return $110$ arcs,
while the explicit bipartite construction has $180$. This is evidence of
a limitation of those runs, not a smaller extremal value and not evidence
against feasibility of the construction.

\subsection{Simple directed hypergraphs}
\label{sec:status-directed-simple-hypergraph}

In the forward model a hyperedge has one tail and $r-1$ heads. For fixed
$r\ge3,m\ge2$, the proposed leading term under both separations is
$(m-1)n^2/(4(r-1))$. The proposed upper proof forms a shadow digraph on
tail--head pairs. Its matching argument accounts for the fact that distinct
two-step routes in the shadow may use the same hyperedge. The candidate
constant is attained asymptotically by a two-layer construction whose heads
have bounded incidence degrees (\Cref{thm:dir-hyper-constant}). These
arguments remain proposed proofs.

At small orders, the finite supply of distinct head sets can prevent that
construction from attaining its asymptotic rate. The bounded-outgoing-degree
family can be larger there. For example, at $n=m=6,r=3$, giving each tail
five distinct outgoing hyperedges produces thirty hyperedges and a direct
degree-cut feasibility check (\Cref{const:bounded-outdegree-hyper}).
The random greedy results measure what the implemented search finds among
these possibilities. They neither establish optimality nor show that edge
and vertex separation have the same finite maximum.

\subsection{Directed multihypergraphs and orientation choices}
\label{sec:status-directed-multihypergraph}\label{sec:orientation-models}

Repeated hyperedges remove the restriction on distinct head sets in the
two-layer lower construction. The proposed leading term remains
$(m-1)n^2/(4(r-1))$ under both separations, although the finite values can
differ from the simple model. At $r=2$ these models are directed
multigraphs. The simple $r=2$ counterpart instead has leading coefficient
$1/4$, so the simple hypergraph conclusion must keep its $r\ge3$ hypothesis.

Orientation introduces another distinction. The backward model has a single
head and is paired with the forward model by reversing all routes. The
general model permits every nonempty tail--head split. The proposed
reversal proof and general-shadow argument are retained in
\Cref{lem:dir-hyper-duality,thm:dir-hyper-general-constant}. They predict the
same leading term for forward, backward and general hypergraphs. This does
not cover a model restricted to one prescribed intermediate tail-set size.
It also does not establish equality of finite forward and general values
once $n>r$.

The plotted experiments use rank three and the forward convention. They do
not constitute an exhaustive comparison of the three orientation models.
Standalone checks of general orientations are described in the appendix.
The remaining tasks are to verify the proposed general bounds and then
determine finite exact values and any next-order differences between models.

\subsection{Reading the finite data}

The audit's grids and tables (\Cref{sec:variant-grids}) supplement this status
map. Completed enumerations, conjectural formula values, supplied
constructions and raw search outcomes have distinct markings. A conjectural
upper-bound curve need not be attained. A completed finite value remains
valid even if a proposed proof of a general formula has not been verified.
The historical grids and equal-allocation experiment use different protocols
and should not be combined into one performance comparison.

\section{Contributions and limitations of the program}

The program gives all sixteen variants a shared interface and an exact
maximum-flow checker. Matrix models use tabu search, and hypergraph models
use random greedy growth. Exhaustion establishes a finite optimum only when
the candidate space is covered by enumeration and valid pruning. Formula
evaluation and heuristic discovery do not provide that coverage.

The equal-allocation experiment tests eight parameter cases per variant,
with three seeds per case. The best run reaches the separately supplied
construction count in $74$ of $128$ cases and falls short in $54$.
All three seeds reach it in $68$ cases, and some runs exceed it. The
comparison therefore measures recovery of supplied constructions, not a
success rate for proving optima. The large directed gaps show why the seven
short validation cases cannot establish reliability at larger sizes.

The repository retains witnesses and timing records for that experiment,
including the excluded originals and their timing replacements. An
independent checker validates the witnesses. Historical grids predate some
of that recording and lack individual witnesses or elapsed times in some
cells. Their reported values can be redrawn, but timed searches need not
repeat identically on another machine. The audit gives the commands and
distinguishes these levels of reproducibility.

Several mathematical questions remain beyond those checks. Agreement on
small instances does not verify a general proposed proof. A feasible graph
does not prove an upper bound. Solver checks without a replayable matching
bound do not replace an argument. The conjectures with proofs
therefore remain separate from the finite computational evidence.

\section{Open problems}

The first task is to verify the conjectures with proofs and their dependencies.
The detailed status discussion also identifies four directions that would
remain open even if those arguments were verified.
\begin{itemize}
\item For directed graphs, determine the exact values, linear corrections and
extremal structures, including whether the two separations agree in the simple model.
\item For undirected vertex separation, determine the simple value at $m\ge6$
and the multigraph value at $m\ge4$, including the best blocks and growth rates.
\item For undirected hypergraphs, determine edge attainment outside the range
where it is established and find vertex bounds beyond the $m=3$ rank argument.
\item For directed hypergraphs, determine finite values and compare the effects
of multiplicity, separation and orientation beyond the proposed leading term.
\end{itemize}
The computations supply examples and finite checks for these questions.
Extending them to arbitrary orders requires a verified general argument.
