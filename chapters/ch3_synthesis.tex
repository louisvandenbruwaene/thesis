This thesis began by proving certain variants using logical arguments,
confirmed those results for small graphs and then searched bigger graphs for dense examples.
This chapter collects the output of those programs, interprets it and provides conclusions for all variants.

\section{The directed frontier}
The directed arc problem is a race between a hub with $m(n-1)$ arcs and
an augmented bipartite construction with $\floor{(n+m-2)^2/4}$ arcs
(\Cref{const:directed-hub,const:augmented-bipartite}). They cross near $n=9$ at $m=3$.

\begin{conjecture}\label{conj:dir-arc}
For simple digraphs and all $n \ge m \ge 2$,
\[
    \ell_m^{\mathrm{dir}}(n) = \max\!\left(m(n-1),\ \floorfrac{(n+m-2)^2}{4}\right).
\]
\end{conjecture}

At $m = 2$ this is \Cref{thm:dir-arc-m2-exact}, and the first branch is the
directed hub of \Cref{const:directed-hub}. \Cref{fig:crossover} plots the two
branches at $m = 3$ and the size at which the second overtakes the first.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{figures/directed_crossover.png}
    \caption{The two lower-bound branches of \Cref{conj:dir-arc} at $m = 3$, and the maximum they define.}
    \label{fig:crossover}
\end{figure}

Exhaustive search proves the values $6,9,12,15$ for $n=3,4,5,6$ when $m=3$, for
both separation conditions (\Cref{rem:dir-vertex-m3}), and does not settle
$n = 7$. Past the sizes exhaustion reaches, a general upper bound would follow
from the structural claim of \Cref{open:decomposition}: that every extremal
example other than the hub splits into a part $A$ and a part $B$ with every arc
running from $A$ to $B$ and none returning. One returning arc is enough to break
the count, since it lets a route leave $B$ through $A$ and exceed the internal
budget of $m-2$. Ruling that arc out for every extremal example is the step that
is missing.

The conjecture is therefore correct in its leading term and in the size of its error. 
This is proved in \Cref{thm:dir-arc-linear-error}, without any assumptions about the shape of the extremal graph. 
The only unknown is the coefficient of the linear error term: 
it lies somewhere between the conjectured $(m-2)/2$ and the proved $4(m-1)$. 
The discussion below considers what the bound can and cannot tell us about the \emph{shape} of an extremal graph. 
A weaker form of the same argument answers that separate question.

The directed vertex problem also has the same leading term and error order, 
by \Cref{thm:dir-vertex-linear-error}. \Cref{app:proofs} treats it separately from the arc problem. 
It remains open whether the two values are exactly equal for $m \ge 3$.

If parallel arcs are allowed, the problem is completely solved: 
\Cref{thm:dir-multi-full} gives the exact value for every $n \ge 2$ and $m \ge 2$.

\section{Why direction makes the value quadratic}

In an undirected graph every edge contributes to the degree of both its
endpoints, and Mader's theorem turns this symmetry into a hard ceiling of
$\floor{m(n-1)/2}$ edges, linear in $n$. A digraph does not have this symmetry.
In \Cref{const:bipartite} all arcs go from one part of the vertices to the other
and none come back, so a route can cross from the first part to the second only
once. That construction holds $\floor{n^2/4}$ arcs at $\lec^{\max} = 1$, and no
undirected graph can carry so many edges at connectivity one once $n$ is large.
The linear ceiling therefore does not survive the change of model: already at
$m = 2$ and $n = 8$ the one-directional construction holds $16$ arcs against the
hub's $14$, so the directed value follows the larger of the two branches rather
than the linear one alone.

\Cref{const:augmented-bipartite} carries the same idea to every $m$ by letting
each vertex of the larger part receive $m - 2$ extra in-arcs from within that
part, drawn in \Cref{fig:augmented-wall}. These arcs provide $m - 2$ short
detours for each pair and raise $\lec^{\max}$ to $m - 1$, while the count
increases to $\floor{(n+m-2)^2/4}$. Its $m = 3$, $n = 10$ instance holds thirty
arcs where the hub branch gives twenty-seven.

The quadratic term is what the directed upper bound has to account for.
The argument must explain why no configuration has more arcs than the bipartite construction.
This requires finding the partition, ruling out arcs in the reverse direction,
and controlling the internal structure of the larger part $B$.
That structural task is what \Cref{open:decomposition} has not yet resolved for $m \ge 3$.
\Cref{prop:dir-arc-stability} and \Cref{thm:dir-arc-linear-error} show that this bipartite structure is the right one,
using two different measures.
In \emph{size}, the extremal value lies within $O_m(n)$ of the wall's count,
so what is left open is a constant inside the second-order term rather than the phenomenon.
In \emph{shape}, every feasible digraph becomes a subgraph of a one-directional bipartite graph
after deleting $O(\sqrt{m}\,n^{3/2})$ arcs,
a weaker error term because it comes from the count that does not induct on a low-degree vertex.
The undirected problem has no analogous obstacle, because Mader's degree-counting argument closes it in a few lines.
The quadratic phenomenon is therefore the reason the directed and undirected problems differ in difficulty.

\subsection{Orientations of a directed hyperedge}
\label{sec:orientation-models}

The directed hypergraph rows of \Cref{tab:summary} rest on one bound,
\Cref{thm:dir-hyper-constant}, which pins the leading term. The table uses the
\emph{forward} convention throughout: a hyperedge has one tail and $r-1$ heads.
Reversing every hyperedge makes the one-head \emph{backward} convention
equivalent to it (\Cref{lem:dir-hyper-duality}), and
\Cref{thm:dir-hyper-general-constant} extends the same leading term to every
other way of splitting a hyperedge into tail and head sets, under both
separations. The orientation therefore adds no asymptotic row to
\Cref{tab:summary}. The conventions may still differ at few vertices, and
whether they agree exactly once $n > r$ is open.

\section{Contributions and limitations of the program}

The program replaced separate methods for each case with one shared approach. 
Before it, each variant required its own computation and hand proof. 
Now the same interface represents every variant in two ways: 
with a multiplicity matrix for graph models and with vertex sets for hypergraph models. 
An exact checker tests connectivity, a certifier proves upper bounds for small cases, 
and a tabu search finds examples that give lower bounds. 
The rediscovery in \Cref{sec:rediscovery} shows that this approach works: 
when applied to solved cases, it recovers the known answers.

These three tools provide different levels of evidence. 
The checker measures a given graph exactly, and a complete search proves the best value for a finite case. 
The certifier is also a finite check, but it is not a reusable proof unless it comes with a replayable bound. 
Heuristic search finds examples and lower bounds only. Thus, a value is settled only when the lower and upper bounds agree. 
Directed vertex values were checked separately from arc values because Whitney's inequality gives a witness, but not an upper bound. 
Every computational claim in this thesis can be rerun from \code{program/erdos915\_unified.py}. 
Important values are also checked with independent implementations, except for two block sweeps in \Cref{app:proofs}. 
Those sweeps use separate scripts in \code{research\_notes/scripts/}, which are named where their results appear. 
\Cref{app:verification-ladder,app:software-tests} gives the environment, commands, and audit evidence. 
No theorem here depends only on an unchecked solver result.

The program also has limits. The search finds examples but does not prove upper bounds. 
The certifier proves upper bounds only for a fixed size, and only as a finite solver check rather than as a reusable certificate (\Cref{sec:certification-standard}). 
The directed multigraph encoding works this way only up to $n = 6$. 
For larger $n$, the value is instead established by the hand proof in \Cref{app:proofs}; at $n = 7$, the certifier ran out of time without giving an answer. 
The generation enumerator's degree-capped classification at $n = 7$ (\Cref{rem:n7-classification}) is verified only for extremisers with maximum total degree at most $8$, not for all graphs. 
These results do not close the structural gaps that remain open; the next section lists them. 
Three of those gaps cannot be solved by computation alone. The decomposition in \Cref{open:decomposition} would prove \Cref{conj:dir-arc}; its backward-arc clause is only one part of that decomposition. 
The directed vertex problem for $m \ge 3$ does not follow from Whitney's inequality and the arc case. 
The undirected vertex-disjoint problem for $m \ge 6$ has been open for half a century, and the program can explore it but not solve it.
